# Patterns that make prose read as machine-written

Each entry says what the pattern looks like and how to fix it. **Strong** means one sighting is
worth fixing. **Weak** means the pattern is ordinary in human writing and counts only when several
tells cluster in the same passage. Quoted examples are for spotting the pattern; never leave them
in output.

Families A, B, D, E and F describe structure and apply in any language. The word lists in C are
English.

## A. Staging instead of stating

**False contrast** (strong). "It's not about speed, it's about trust." / "This isn't a tool, it's
a mindset." / "not just X, but Y". Also the split form ("This does not mean we stop. It means we
slow down.") and the clipped tail (", no guesswork"). State the real claim: "Users stay because
they trust the numbers." Keep a contrast only when it corrects something the reader actually
believes.

**One-line closers and fragment rows** (strong). "And that changes everything." / "Let that sink
in." / "That's the real win." / "No meetings. No drift. Just shipping." / "every. single. day." or
words in ALL CAPS for weight. Cut a closer that repeats or explains; join fragments into one
sentence that makes a specific claim.

**Sayings that sound deep** (strong). "At its core", "the real question is", "what really
matters", "the heart of the matter", "X is the new Y", "not a feature but a philosophy". Replace
with the specific claim, or cut.

**Teaser setup** (strong). "Here's the thing:", "What nobody tells you is…", "The secret?",
"Let's dive in", "Let's break it down", "Without further ado", "Real talk:", "Honestly?" as a
standalone opener. Delete the setup; the claim stands alone. A plain "honestly" inside a sentence
is fine.

**Colon reveal** (strong). "The result: a 3x faster build." Write a sentence: "The build is now
three times faster." Keep colons for lists, labels and quotes.

**Rhetorical question answered at once** (strong). "Why does this matter? Because…". State the
answer.

**Arguing with no one** (strong). "To be clear", "I'm not saying…", "Don't get me wrong", "You
might think X, but…", "A tempting approach would be…", "This isn't about…". Remove the objection
nobody raised. Keep an objection the text attributes to someone and answers.

**Reader steering** (strong). "This is key.", "Notice that…", "Importantly,", "As you can see,".
Show why it matters instead of announcing that it does.

## B. Rhythm by rule

**Reflex triplets** (strong). Three adjectives, examples or clauses because three sounds complete
("fast, simple, and reliable"). Keep as many items as are true and specific; two or four is fine.

**Repeated openings** (strong when three or more in a row). "We… We… We…", "She… She… She…".
Merge sentences, change the subject or lead with the action. Deliberate repetition for rhythm
stays.

**Staccato drama** (strong). "We built it. We shipped it. It worked." Join into a normal sentence
unless the rhythm is the writer's own.

**Dashes as the universal joint** (weak). Em dashes, en dashes and " -- " doing the work of commas,
colons and full stops. None in a short message; one or two in long text, where a comma or full
stop reads worse. Break up any cluster. Code, ranges and a writer's sample that uses dashes are
exempt.

**Hedge stacking** (weak). "might potentially perhaps help", "could arguably", "in some cases it
may". One hedge, and only where the uncertainty is real. Plain "perhaps" or "tends to" is not a
tell, and scope, legal and safety caveats stay.

**Intensifiers** (weak). *really, very, truly, actually, simply, just, literally, incredibly,
extremely, fundamentally, essentially*. Keep one when it carries real contrast or the writer's
speech rhythm.

**Hyphen pairs everywhere** (weak). "high-quality", "long-term", "client-facing" after the noun as
well as before it. Hyphenate before the noun only; fixed forms like "third-party" stay.

**Passive voice and missing subjects** (weak). "No setup required.", "Mistakes were made." Name
the actor where it makes the sentence clearer.

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

**Stock words** (strong when several appear; one alone is weak). Replace with the plain word, or
cut:

| Stock word | Plain choice |
|---|---|
| delve into, dive into, deep dive | look at, cover |
| leverage, utilize | use |
| facilitate, empower, enable | help, let |
| streamline | simplify, speed up |
| enhance, elevate, bolster, supercharge | improve |
| robust, seamless, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art | say what it does |
| pivotal, paramount, crucial, vital, key (adj.) | important, or show why |
| transformative, game-changing, revolutionary, groundbreaking | say what changed |
| landscape, realm, tapestry, ecosystem, interplay (figurative) | field, area, or cut |
| embark on, unlock, garner | start, get |
| meticulous, intricate, multifaceted | careful, detailed, or show it |
| navigate (figurative), foster, harness, align with | handle, build, use, match |
| highlight, underscore, showcase (verbs) | show, or cut |
| additionally | also, or cut |
| valuable, vibrant, enduring | say what makes it so |
| quietly (as in "quietly reshaping") | cut |

A formal word not on this list is not a tell by itself.

**Empty phrases** (strong). Cut and start with the content: *it's worth noting that, it's
important to remember, when it comes to, in today's fast-paced world, at the end of the day, in
terms of, the fact that, in order to (→ to), as a matter of fact, needless to say*.

**Inflated significance** (strong). "marks a turning point", "is a testament to", "plays a key
role in", "underscores the importance of", "sets the stage for", "leaves a lasting legacy", "the
future looks bright", "despite challenges, it continues to thrive". Give the fact and let the
reader judge. End on the last concrete fact.

**Hollow -ing tail** (strong). "…, highlighting our commitment to quality." / "…, reflecting the
team's values." / "…, ensuring a smooth experience." Replace with the concrete consequence or cut.

**Sales language** (strong). "nestled in", "in the heart of", "renowned", "breathtaking",
"stunning", "a diverse array of", "rich" (figurative), "boasts". State what the thing is and has.

**Avoiding is and has** (strong). "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "represents",
"boasts", "features", "offers". Use "is", "are", "has".

**Vague attribution** (strong). "Studies show", "experts agree", "many believe", "industry
reports". Name the source and what it said. If there is none, cut the claim or ask the user for
one. A missing citation alone is not a tell.

**Vague connection** (weak). "associated with", "linked to", "tied to". Name the relationship if
you know it; otherwise leave it.

**Fake-active subjects** (weak). "The data tells a story", "The roadmap reflects our ambition".
Give the action to a person: "We planned three releases."

## D. Formatting by rule

**Formatting a short text** (strong). Headings, bullets and bold only when the text is long enough
to need navigation. A short message is prose.

**Bold labels and decorative bold** (strong). Bold scattered through sentences, or "**Label:**"
on every list item. Remove the bold; turn the list into prose when the labels add nothing.

**Decorative headings** (strong). Title Case, emoji or arrows in headings and list items, a rule
between every section, a heading that repeats the title. Use sentence case and name what the
section holds.

**Colon case** (weak). After a colon, continue in lower case unless a proper noun, a full quoted
sentence or code follows.

**Curly quotes** (weak). Use straight quotes where the target format does.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

**Chatbot residue** (strong, the most certain tell). "Great question!", "Certainly!", "Of
course!", "You're absolutely right", "I hope this helps", "Here is a…", "Want me to…?", "Would you
like…?". Remove the wrapper and keep the content.

**Knowledge-limit disclaimers** (strong). "as of my last update", "based on available
information", "details are limited", "it is believed that", "likely grew up in…". Say what the
source does not show, or cut the sentence.

**Heading restated** (strong). A heading followed by a first sentence that repeats it ("## Pricing
/ This section covers pricing."). Delete the restatement.

**Writing about the document** (strong; a single line is weak). "This section is organised
by…", "The table below compares…", "This was added to replace the previous approach", "compiled
from several sources". Describe the subject instead. Keep a source credit the reader can follow
and a caveat that changes what the reader should do.

**Announcing and restating** (strong). "In this post I will explain…", "Below you'll find…", or
the same point again after "In other words" or "Put simply". Explain; keep the clearer version.

**Over-politeness** (strong). Thanking, apologising and offering further help in one message, or
"I hope this message finds you well", "please don't hesitate to reach out", "feel free to". One
line at most, and only where the relationship needs it.

**Endings** (strong). A recap ("In summary…", "Overall…", a last paragraph repeating the body) or
a mic-drop aphorism ("And that makes all the difference."). Cut it; end on the last concrete
sentence or the next step. "Let me know if you have any questions!" stays only when the channel
expects it, once.

**Generic context** (strong). "Communication is key in any team." Fails the portability test.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

**Re-explaining what the reader knows** (strong in a reply). The reply restates the problem,
walks through the diagnosis and shows its evidence, and the decision arrives in the last line.
Lead with the decision; keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees; move
the rest to a ticket or doc. Act on this only when the text is plainly a reply or the conversation
around it is visible; otherwise ask.
